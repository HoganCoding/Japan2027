Omoide Yokocho (思い出横丁, “Memory Lane”)

A narrow alley next to Shinjuku Station that grew out of a post-WWII black market. Dozens of tiny yakitori stalls with only a few seats each, charcoal smoke and red lanterns. Locals jokingly call it “Piss Alley” because it once had no toilets.

Hours: about 17:00 until late · Allow: 30–60 min · Note: stalls are tiny, a group of 4 may have to wait; some charge a seating fee.

Loading photo…

Kabukicho and the Godzilla head (歌舞伎町)

Tokyo’s biggest nightlife district, full of neon signs. A life-size Godzilla head sits on top of Hotel Gracery, visible from Kabukicho Ichibangai street. Near Shinjuku Station’s East Exit, a curved billboard shows a giant 3D cat.

Allow: 20–30 min · Note: stay on the main streets; ignore touts.

Loading photo…

Tokyo Metropolitan Government Building (東京都庁)

Twin towers 243 m tall, designed by architect Kenzo Tange. The 45th-floor observatory is free and gives a full view of Tokyo at night. On clear days (usually in winter) you can see Mount Fuji.

Hours: about 9:30–22:00 · Allow: 30–45 min.

Quick photo spots

  • Omoide Yokocho: red lantern alley, shot from the station end.
  • Godzilla head: from Kabukicho Ichibangai street.
  • 3D cat: Cross Shinjuku Vision screen, Shinjuku Station East Exit.

About the places

Loading photo…

Meiji Jingu (明治神宮)

Tokyo’s largest Shinto shrine, built in 1920 for Emperor Meiji and Empress Shoken. It sits in a forest of about 100,000 trees donated from all over Japan. Past the giant wooden torii is a wall of sake barrels offered by brewers. Traditional weddings are common on Sunday mornings.

Hours: sunrise to sunset · Allow: 60 min · Admission: free.

Loading photo…

Takeshita Street (竹下通り, Harajuku)

A pedestrian street about 350 m long, the center of Tokyo youth fashion. Famous for crepes, giant cotton candy and “kawaii” shops. Very crowded on weekends.

Allow: 30–60 min · Tip: go before 11:00 to avoid the crush.

Loading photo…

Shibuya Crossing (渋谷スクランブル交差点)

Often called the world’s busiest pedestrian crossing: at each green light, up to thousands of people cross in every direction. An icon of modern Tokyo seen in many films.

Allow: 20–30 min · Tip: cross once or twice, then go up high (Shibuya Sky, Starbucks Tsutaya, MAGNET by Shibuya109) for the overhead shot.

Loading photo…

Hachiko statue (ハチ公)

Bronze statue of the loyal Akita dog who waited at Shibuya Station for his owner every day for nearly 10 years after the owner died (1925–1935). The story became the film Hachi: A Dog’s Tale (2009). A classic Tokyo meeting point.

Allow: 5–10 min · Note: there is often a line for photos.

Loading photo…

teamLab Planets (Toyosu)

An immersive digital art museum: you walk barefoot, wade knee-deep through water with projected koi, stand in a room of floating flowers and in an infinite mirror room. Scheduled to close at the end of 2027, so 2027 is the last chance.

Allow: 60–90 min · Note: wear pants that roll up above the knee; free lockers. Light-colored clothes look best in photos.

Loading photo…

Shibuya Sky (渋谷スカイ)

Open-air rooftop on the 47th floor of Shibuya Scramble Square, about 230 m up, looking straight down on Shibuya Crossing. At sunset Tokyo shifts from gold to city lights; on clear days Mount Fuji is visible.

Hours: about 10:00–22:30 · Allow: 60 min · Note: timed tickets; sunset slots sell out fast. No bags on the roof; lockers available.

Quick photo spots

  • Sake barrel wall on the path into Meiji Jingu.
  • Takeshita Street gate seen from Harajuku Station.
  • Shibuya Crossing from above: Starbucks Tsutaya 2nd floor (free, crowded) or the MAGNET by Shibuya109 rooftop.
  • teamLab: the koi water room and the floating flower room.
  • Shibuya Sky: the glass corner at the building’s edge.

About the places

Tsukiji Outer Market (築地場外市場)

The wholesale fish market moved to Toyosu in 2018, but the outer market with about 400 stalls stayed. It is the place for a fresh seafood breakfast: sushi, seafood rice bowls, hot tamagoyaki, grilled scallops, strawberries and dorayaki.

Hours: about 6:00–14:00 (many stalls closed Sundays and some Wednesdays) · Allow: 1.5 hours · Note: eat standing at the stall, not while walking.

Loading photo…

Hama-rikyu Gardens (浜離宮恩賜庭園)

A 350-year-old garden of the Tokugawa family, surrounded by Shiodome skyscrapers. Its seawater pond rises and falls with the tide, and the Nakajima teahouse in the middle serves matcha with Japanese sweets.

Hours: about 9:00–17:00 · Allow: 45–60 min.

Loading photo…

Senso-ji (浅草寺, Asakusa)

Tokyo’s oldest temple, founded in 628 and dedicated to Kannon. Enter through the Kaminarimon gate with its giant red lantern (about 700 kg), then walk Nakamise street, about 250 m of souvenir and snack stalls. You can draw an omikuji fortune for ¥100.

Hours: main hall about 6:00–17:00; grounds always open · Allow: 1–1.5 hours.

Loading photo…

Azumabashi bridge (吾妻橋)

A red bridge over the Sumida River, 5 minutes’ walk from Senso-ji. From here you see Tokyo Skytree (634 m, Japan’s tallest tower) and the Asahi building with its “golden flame” on top.

Allow: 10–15 min.

Loading photo…

Akihabara (秋葉原)

“Electric Town” and the capital of anime, manga and game culture. Multi-story shops for figures, trading cards, retro games (Super Potato), electronics (Yodobashi Akiba) and arcades.

Hours: most shops 10:00/11:00–20:00 · Allow: 2–3 hours.

Quick photo spots

  • Tamagoyaki stall at Tsukiji: video the cook flipping the omelet.
  • Kaminarimon gate: shoot from across the street to fit the whole lantern.
  • Senso-ji pagoda with Skytree in one frame, from the right side of the main hall.
  • Azumabashi bridge: Skytree, the Asahi building and the Sumida River.
  • Akihabara: the Radio Kaikan building and Chuo-dori lights.

Nishiki Market (錦市場, “Kyoto’s Kitchen”)

A covered market street about 400 m long with over 100 stalls and 400 years of history. Try: baby octopus stuffed with quail egg, fried tofu, tsukemono pickles, fish cakes, matcha ice cream, mochi.

Hours: about 10:00–17:00 (some stalls closed Wednesdays) · Allow: 1–1.5 hours · Note: no eating while walking; eat at the stall.

Loading photo…

Kiyomizu-dera (清水寺, “Pure Water Temple”)

UNESCO World Heritage site, founded in 778. Famous for its large wooden stage jutting out over the hillside, 13 m high, built without a single nail. Below, the Otowa waterfall splits into three streams said to bring health, love and success in study.

Hours: about 6:00–18:00 · Allow: 1–1.5 hours · Note: uphill walk; busy in the afternoon.

Loading photo…

Sannenzaka, Ninenzaka and Yasaka Pagoda (産寧坂・二寧坂・八坂の塔)

Stone-paved slopes lined with traditional wooden houses, linking Kiyomizu-dera down to Gion. Along the way: tea houses, pottery, fans, and a Starbucks in an old wooden townhouse. At the bottom is the five-story Yasaka Pagoda (Hokan-ji), one of Kyoto’s most classic photo views.

Allow: 45–60 min · Note: legend says tripping on Sannenzaka brings bad luck, so watch your step.

Loading photo…

Yasaka Shrine (八坂神社)

A Shinto shrine over 1,350 years old at the end of Shijo street, home of the famous Gion Matsuri festival every July. Its orange west gate is the landmark of Gion. Hundreds of lanterns light up in the evening.

Hours: always open · Allow: 20–30 min · Admission: free.

Loading photo…

Gion, Shirakawa and Hanamikoji (祇園)

Japan’s most famous geisha district. Shirakawa is a small canal lined with willows and old wooden houses; Hanamikoji is a stone street of traditional teahouses. At dusk you may see geiko and maiko (apprentice geisha) on their way to work.

Allow: 1 hour · Note: don’t take close-up photos, don’t block their path, and don’t enter private lanes (signs posted; fines of ¥10,000 possible).

Loading photo…

Pontocho (先斗町)

A narrow lane about 500 m long running alongside the Kamo River, lined with restaurants and teahouses. From May, many build “kawayuka” wooden terraces over the river.

Allow: 30 min · Note: many places are expensive and need reservations; a stroll is enough.

Quick photo spots

  • From the Shinkansen window (seat E): Mount Fuji, around the Fuji River near Shin-Fuji Station.
  • Kiyomizu-dera: the wooden stage, shot from the south path (past Okunoin hall).
  • Yasaka Pagoda seen from the Yasaka-dori slope.
  • Starbucks Ninenzaka: traditional wooden house with noren curtains.
  • Tatsumi Bridge (Gion Shirakawa): old wooden houses by the canal, willow trees.

About the places

Loading photo…

Fushimi Inari Taisha (伏見稲荷大社)

Head shrine of about 30,000 shrines to Inari, the god of rice, founded in 711. Famous for about 10,000 vermilion torii gates donated by businesses and individuals, forming tunnels up Mount Inari. The fox statues everywhere are the god’s messengers.

Hours: 24 hours · Allow: 1–2.5 hours depending on how far you climb · Admission: free · Note: arrive before 7:30 for empty paths.

Loading photo…

Nanzen-ji (南禅寺)

One of Japan’s most important Zen temples, founded in 1291. The 22 m Sanmon gate has a balcony with city views. On the grounds stands the Suirokaku aqueduct, a red-brick Roman-style structure from the Meiji era, striking among the old temple buildings.

Hours: about 8:40–17:00 · Allow: 1 hour · Admission: grounds free.

Loading photo…

Philosopher’s Path (哲学の道)

A stone path along a small canal, about 2 km from Nanzen-ji to Ginkaku-ji. Named after philosopher Nishida Kitaro, who walked here to think. Lined with cherry trees, small cafes and temples. Green and quiet in early June.

Allow: 30–60 min · Note: flat and easy, good for parents.

Loading photo…

Ginkaku-ji, the Silver Pavilion (銀閣寺)

Retirement villa of shogun Ashikaga Yoshimasa, built in 1482 and later turned into a Zen temple. Despite the name, it was never covered in silver. Known for its raked white sand garden, the tall sand cone called the “moon-viewing platform”, and a hillside path with views over the grounds.

Hours: about 8:30–17:00 · Allow: 45–60 min.

Quick photo spots

  • Senbon Torii: go 5–10 minutes past the first section for fewer people.
  • Suirokaku aqueduct: European brick arches inside a Japanese temple.
  • Philosopher’s Path: canal, trees and wooden houses.
  • Ginkaku-ji: the white sand cone, and the pavilion seen from the hillside path.

Arashiyama Bamboo Grove (嵐山竹林の小径)

A path about 400 m long through towering bamboo. The rustling sound in the wind is listed by Japan’s Ministry of the Environment among “100 soundscapes to preserve”. Packed during the day; only early morning feels peaceful.

Hours: always open · Allow: 20–30 min · Admission: free.

Loading photo…

Tenryu-ji (天龍寺)

Arashiyama’s head Zen temple, UNESCO World Heritage site, founded in 1339. Its Sogenchi pond garden, with the Arashiyama mountains as a backdrop, is one of Japan’s oldest gardens and keeps much of its 14th-century design. The back gate leads straight into the bamboo grove.

Hours: about 8:30–17:00 · Allow: 45–60 min.

Loading photo…

Okochi Sanso (大河内山荘)

The villa and large garden of samurai film star Denjiro Okochi, built over about 30 years. A hillside path looks down over Kyoto and the Hozu gorge. Admission includes a bowl of matcha and a sweet at the teahouse at the end.

Hours: about 9:00–17:00 · Allow: 45–60 min · Note: gentle slopes, many steps.

Loading photo…

Togetsukyo bridge (渡月橋, “Moon-Crossing Bridge”)

A 155 m wooden bridge over the Katsura River, the symbol of Arashiyama. The name comes from a 13th-century emperor who said the moon seemed to be crossing it. Nearby, Randen Arashiyama Station has the Kimono Forest: about 600 pillars wrapped in colorful kimono fabric.

Allow: 20–30 min.

Loading photo…

Kinkaku-ji, the Golden Pavilion (金閣寺)

Kyoto’s most famous sight, UNESCO World Heritage site. The top two floors are covered in gold leaf and reflected in the Kyokochi pond. The original from 1397 was burned by a monk in 1950; the current building was rebuilt in 1955.

Hours: about 9:00–17:00 · Allow: 45 min · Note: one-way route; the best photo spot is right at the start.

Quick photo spots

  • Bamboo grove: arrive before 8:00; shoot upward toward the tops.
  • Togetsukyo bridge with green hills behind, from the north riverbank.
  • Kimono Forest inside Randen Arashiyama Station.
  • Kinkaku-ji: the first pond-side spot after the gate, with the reflection.

Nara Park (奈良公園)

A park of about 500 hectares in Nara, Japan’s capital in the 8th century. About 1,200 deer roam freely; they are considered messengers of the gods and are a national natural treasure. They have learned to bow for senbei crackers.

Hours: always open · Allow: 30–45 min · Note: hungry deer may butt or nip; don’t tease them, and show empty hands when you’re out of crackers.

Loading photo…

Todai-ji (東大寺)

UNESCO World Heritage site, built in 752. The Great Buddha Hall (Daibutsuden) was once the world’s largest wooden building and houses a bronze Buddha about 15 m tall. Inside is a pillar with a hole the size of the Buddha’s nostril; squeezing through is said to bring enlightenment.

Hours: about 7:30–17:30 · Allow: 1 hour.

Loading photo…

Kasuga Taisha (春日大社)

Shinto shrine founded in 768, famous for about 3,000 stone and bronze lanterns lining the paths and hanging around the shrine. The approach runs through old forest, cool and quiet.

Hours: about 6:30–17:30 · Allow: 45 min.

Dotonbori (道頓堀)

Himeji Castle (姫路城, “White Heron Castle”)

Japan’s finest and best-preserved original castle, and the country’s first UNESCO site (1993). The six-story main keep was finished in 1609, its white walls like a heron’s wings. It was never destroyed, even when the city was bombed in 1945. Inside is the original wooden structure, with steep stairs, arrow slits and rock-dropping chutes.

Hours: about 9:00–17:00 · Allow: 1.5–2 hours · Note: shoes off inside the keep; bring socks; very steep stairs.

Loading photo…

Koko-en Garden (好古園)

Nine small Japanese gardens built in 1992 on the site of former samurai residences, right next to the castle. Koi ponds, a waterfall, a bamboo garden and a teahouse, with the castle in the background.

Hours: about 9:00–17:00 · Allow: 30–45 min.

Quick photo spots

  • Otemae avenue: from Himeji Station’s north exit, the white castle sits at the end of the street.
  • Sannomaru Square: the full castle view, the most classic shot.
  • From the top floor of the keep: over the city and the tiled roofs.
  • From the Shinkansen window (seat E): Mount Fuji, and a live check on tomorrow’s weather.

Mount Fuji (富士山)

Japan’s highest mountain at 3,776 m, an active volcano (last eruption in 1707). UNESCO cultural heritage site since 2013 and a sacred symbol in Japanese religion and art. The Fuji Five Lakes area at its northern foot has the best views.

Note: often hidden by clouds in June; early morning is clearest.

Loading photo…

Chureito Pagoda (忠霊塔, Arakura Sengen Shrine)

A red five-story pagoda on the Arakura hillside, built in 1963 as a memorial to Fujiyoshida residents who died in war. The view of the red pagoda with Mount Fuji behind it is one of Japan’s most famous images.

Hours: always open · Allow: 1–1.5 hours · Admission: free · Easiest way up: taxi to the shrine, then the zigzag slope path (15–20 min) instead of 398 steps; the viewing deck is above the pagoda.

Practical tips

  • Cash: carry about ¥30,000–50,000 ($200–333). Small restaurants, temples and markets are often cash only. Use 7-Eleven ATMs. When paying by card, choose to pay in yen.
  • Jet lag: Houston–Tokyo is 14 hours apart. Light dinner and early bed on Day 1. You’ll wake early the first few days; use it to beat the crowds.
  • Popular restaurants: arrive 15–20 min before opening or after 14:00.
  • Shrines and temples: Shinto shrines have torii gates; Buddhist temples have Buddha statues and incense. Rinse your hands at the water basin before entering; no photos inside halls where signs say so.
  • Himeji Castle: wear easy-off shoes and bring socks; the stairs are very steep.
  • Rain: folding umbrella and quick-dry shoes. In heavy rain on a Kyoto day, swap Arashiyama for Nishiki Market and the Kyoto Railway Museum.
  • Shoes: expect 15,000–20,000 steps a day.
  • Trash: few public bins; carry a small bag and take trash back to the hotel.
  • Tax-free: bring your passport when spending over ¥5,000 ($33) in major stores.
  • Internet: Japan eSIM (Ubigi, Airalo) or your US carrier’s international plan.
  • Oversized luggage on the Shinkansen: bags over 160 cm (total of 3 dimensions) need a seat with oversized baggage space. This plan forwards suitcases by takkyubin, so it doesn’t apply.
